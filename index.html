01 — Why CRMs get abandoned

The problem is rarely the software. It's the model.

A

Stages nobody agreed on

Marketing calls it a lead, sales calls it a contact, the owner calls it "that lady from Tuesday". When reports disagree, people stop opening them.

What I doI define each stage by the event, not the calendar. One owner and one entry rule per stage.

Real work SBT: aligned the teams involved and ran campaigns end to end.

B

Data with no owner

Duplicates, blank fields and the real conversations happening on WhatsApp, outside the system. The CRM ends up knowing less than the person at the counter.

What I doI audit every source, field and duplicate, and give each field an owner.

Real work Brasil Futebol Expo: organized scattered data from several universities and generated millions in active sales.

C

Automation before process

A drip campaign built on an undefined funnel sends the wrong message to the right person, on schedule. That's a quick way to teach customers to ignore you.

What I doNo journey goes live without a stage, a goal and an exit.

Real work Testing method applied before any commercial action on large projects.

03 — Lifecycle model

Every stage has an entry rule. No exceptions.

  1. 01

    Subscriber

    Opted in. Interested, not yet in the market.

  2. 02

    Lead

    Identified contact with a known source.

  3. 03

    MQL

    Fits the customer profile or took a high-intent action.

  4. 04

    SQL

    Accepted by the owner within 24 hours.

  5. 05

    Opportunity

    Deal created with amount and close date.

  6. 06

    Customer

    First purchase closed. The onboarding clock starts.

  7. 07

    Repeat

    Second purchase inside the expected window.

  8. 08

    Advocate

    Referral, review or testimonial logged.

Rule 1 · Stages move forward on events, automatically. Moving a contact back requires a written reason.

Rule 2 · No owner, no source, no stage: the contact doesn't get in. Clean data is a door policy.

Rule 3 · For a business like the one in this example, the second purchase is the conversion to watch. Repeat and Advocate are where small businesses grow, and where they rarely measure.

05 — Win-back journey

One journey, written down like a contract.

Trigger · event

Customer misses their own usual repurchase date

event: days_since_order > usual_interval × 1.5

Branch · RFV

High value and bought often?

Yes · high value

Task to owner: a personal WhatsApp within 48 hours. A real person, by name, no template.

wait 7 days

No reply: the owner calls. The outcome is logged on the contact.

No · standard

Email 1: "We saved your usual." Their own history, ready to reorder in one tap.

wait 5 days

Email 2: one offer, one deadline, one button.

Goal

New purchase → back to active, exit journey

Exit

Silent for 21 days → marked dormant and left alone for 90. Fewer messages keep the list warm.

Run by @Win-back. A/B test: "your usual" vs. a discount. Primary KPI: win-back rate.

06 — Mobile-first product · concept

Designed for the thumb, between two customers.

Whether on WhatsApp or a dedicated app, owners run the business standing up, between two customers. Gina asks for no dashboard and no new software: she hands over 3 ready decisions, one thumb away.

Behavior rules per screen

B = MAP
Motivation comes from the event, ability from a single tap, the prompt from timing. If one is missing, the screen doesn't ship.
Hook loop
Push → one tap → a real customer replies → the owner tags a preference, and Gina gets better next week.
Loss framing
"3 regulars at risk" is expected to move more owners than "3 opportunities". Same data, different decision. Worth an A/B test.
Endowment
The organised list and saved preferences grow every week. The progress bar makes that investment visible.
Defaults
The recommended action is pre-filled. Changing it is allowed, never required.
Notification budget
One push a day at most, and only when it points to money or a person.

Omnichannel orchestration

In-app

Push

07 — CRM maturity

Four levels. Most businesses skip the first one.

  1. L1

    Organised

    One source of truth. Every contact has an owner, a source and a stage.

  2. L2

    Responsive

    Every inbound message has a reply-time target. Nothing waits more than a business day.

  3. L3

    Proactive

    Onboarding, follow-up and win-back run on events, without anyone having to remember.

  4. L4

    Predictive

    RFV segments and a churn risk score tell the owner who to call this week, before they leave.

08 — What I measure

6 numbers, defined before the first dashboard.

Speed to lead

first reply − first message

Usually one of the cheapest conversion levers a small business has. Interest fades fast.

Stage conversion

entered stage n+1 ÷ entered stage n

Shows exactly where the funnel leaks, so you fix one stage instead of everything.

Repeat purchase rate

customers with 2+ orders ÷ all customers

A strong activation signal for retail and services. Compare it with your own history before treating it as the goal.

Churn rate

customers past their expected return ÷ active customers

Measured against each customer's own rhythm, not a fixed calendar.

Customer lifetime value

avg order value × orders per year × years retained

Tells you how much a customer is worth keeping, and how much to spend getting one.

Data health

% contacts with owner + source + stage

If this one is low, every other number on this list is wrong.

10 — Self-critique

Where this method falls short.

The method above assumes a team. For a business of one, parts of it are too heavy, and a method that can't admit that isn't mature yet. These are the gaps I see and what I'd change.

01Eight stages is too many for a solo owner.

With no sales team, MQL and SQL are the same person on the same afternoon.

Fix → Four stages: New, Talking, Bought, Came back.

02Ninety days is too long to wait for proof.

A micro business decides in the first week whether something is worth its time. Motivation fades faster than the project plan.

Fix → Ship one visible win in seven days: a list of customers to call back.

03It assumes a paid CRM.

Many of these businesses run on WhatsApp and a notebook. A monthly licence is a real cost and a real reason to quit.

Fix → Start in a spreadsheet. Migrate only when the sheet starts to hurt.

04Percentages lie at low volume.

With 40 customers, one sale moves a conversion rate by several points and the dashboard tells a story that isn't true.

Fix → Report counts and names until volume justifies rates.

05WhatsApp is treated as a channel.

For this audience it is the database. The CRM is the copy, and usually an outdated one.

Fix → Design the model around the conversation, not around a form.

06It depends on me to keep running.

If the playbook needs the consultant every month, it has failed at the one thing it was for.

Fix → Hand over a 15-minute weekly ritual the owner can run alone.

Gina Zero. R$0

The same retention logic on free tools: an AI chat bot playing Gina, a spreadsheet as the CRM and one weekly message that says who to call. It costs time, not money, and it gets a business to maturity level 2 without a licence.

Free API. On WhatsApp: labels and quick replies in the free Business app, by hand.

Glue

Apps Script or n8n

Free triggers that log each chat to the sheet and flag who missed their usual date.

Report

One message a week

Who to call, who went quiet, what sold. Three lines the owner reads on a Monday.

Four weeks

Week 1
Copy the sheet template. Add every customer you can remember. Imperfect is fine.
Week 2
Connect the bot. Every chat summary lands as a row, automatically.
Week 3
Switch on the missed-date flag and send the first win-back messages by hand.
Week 4
First weekly report. Keep what got replies, drop the rest.

Honest limits

  • Free tiers have usage caps and can change terms without notice.
  • The official WhatsApp API is paid. At zero budget, WhatsApp stays manual.
  • Customer data still needs consent under LGPD and GDPR, even in a spreadsheet.
  • No uptime guarantee. If the bot is down, the owner is back to the notebook.
